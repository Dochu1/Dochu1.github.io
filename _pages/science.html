When modelling geophysical fluid flows, one inevitably encounters motions which cannot be resolved, yet which feed back on the resolved dynamics. We introduce stochasticity into fluid models through variational principles, so that the resulting models retain the conservation laws and circulation dynamics of their deterministic counterparts. We then construct numerical methods which preserve the same structure at the discrete level.

Modelling

Stochastic modelling of fluids

The effects of unresolved scales motivate stochastic fluid models, and Hamilton's principle shows how noise may be introduced without destroying their geometric structure. Stochastic advection by Lie transport (SALT) preserves Kelvin circulation and the Casimirs, whilst stochastic forcing by Lie transport (SFLT) preserves energy. Applied to the Craik–Leibovich equations, SFLT yields a stochastic vortex force through which the noise in the waves feeds into the ocean currents.

Demos

Each demonstration integrates the same realisation of the noise, or the same driving rough path, with a structure-preserving scheme and with a standard alternative. One may thus observe directly which conservation laws survive discretisation. The simulations run in the browser.

Stochastic multisymplectic PDEs

We consider the nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) equation with stochastic transport or stochastic dispersion as a stochastic multisymplectic partial differential equation (PDE). The Preissmann box scheme, which is the midpoint rule in both time and space, preserves the discrete 2-form conservation law and the mass to machine precision, together with the momentum for linear problems. A linearly implicit scheme, which treats the cubic nonlinearity explicitly, loses both the 2-form and the mass conservation.

Rough-path-driven adjoint systems

We recover the initial state of a pendulum driven by a sample path of fractional Brownian motion (fBm) from noisy observations, using gradients computed by adjoint sweeps. The rough symplectic partitioned Runge–Kutta (RSPRK) pairing preserves the adjoint quasi-conservation law to machine precision, and therefore returns the exact gradient of the discrete loss. A non-symplectic explicit pairing breaks this law and yields biased gradients, with which gradient descent stalls away from a stationary point.
