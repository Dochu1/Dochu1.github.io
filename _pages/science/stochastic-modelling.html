How do we add randomness to the equations of fluid motion without destroying the physics they encode? This page introduces the geometric approach to stochastic modelling that runs through my work, using Stochastic effects of waves on currents in the ocean mixed layer (D. D. Holm and R. Hu, J. Math. Phys. 62, 073102, 2021; arXiv:2011.12094) as a guide.

Why stochastic models?

Ocean and atmosphere models can only resolve motions down to the grid scale, yet the unresolved scales feed back on the resolved flow. In the ocean mixing layer, for example, surface gravity waves drive Langmuir circulations: pairs of counter-rotating vortex tubes aligned roughly with the wind. They entrain sediment and detritus into the mixed layer and affect how oil spills disperse. These waves are fast and small compared with the currents, so most models average over them. Averaging the stratified Euler equations over the wave period gives the Craik–Leibovich (CL) equations, in which the waves act on the current through the Stokes drift and a vortex force.

Averaging, however, discards variability. A stochastic model keeps a statistical description of what has been averaged away: each solution is one plausible realisation, an ensemble of solutions quantifies uncertainty, and the ensemble can be compared with, and corrected by, observations through data assimilation. As Ed Lorenz put it, "climate is what you expect, weather is what you get." The modelling question is then:

What types of noise can be added to fluid dynamics while preserving energy conservation, Kelvin–Noether circulation dynamics, and the conservation laws that come from invariance under relabelling of Lagrangian fluid parcels?

Geometry first: Hamilton's principle and circulation

Ideal fluid dynamics follows from Hamilton's principle \(\delta S = 0\) for an action \(S = \int \ell(u,a)\,dt\). The Lagrangian depends on the Eulerian velocity \(u\) and on advected quantities \(a\), such as buoyancy or mass density, which are carried along by the flow. The fluid Lagrangian is unchanged if we relabel the fluid parcels. Reduction by this symmetry gives the Euler–Poincaré equations

$$ \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\delta \ell}{\delta u} + \mathrm{ad}^*_u \frac{\delta \ell}{\delta u} - \frac{\delta \ell}{\delta a}\diamond a = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}a + \mathcal{L}_u a = 0, $$

where \(\mathcal{L}_u\) is the Lie derivative along \(u\). Noether's theorem for the relabelling symmetry is the Kelvin–Noether circulation theorem. For the incompressible Euler equations it is Kelvin's theorem: the circulation \(\oint_{c(u)} \mathbf{u}\cdot d\mathbf{x}\) around any loop moving with the fluid is conserved. On the Hamiltonian side, the same equations are a Lie–Poisson system for the momentum \(m = \delta\ell/\delta u\) with Hamiltonian \(h(m)\). Energy and circulation are two faces of this single geometric structure, so any modification of the equations should be made inside it.

Two geometric ways to add noise

Noise in the transport: SALT

Stochastic Advection by Lie Transport (SALT), introduced by Holm in 2015, makes the Lagrangian paths of fluid parcels stochastic. The resolved drift velocity \(u\) is augmented by spatial noise modes \(\xi_k(\mathbf{x})\), which represent the effect of the unresolved scales:

$$ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}_t = \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\,dt + \sum_k \boldsymbol{\xi}_k(\mathbf{x}_t)\circ dW^k_t . $$

Everything is then transported along these paths: momentum, buoyancy and any other advected quantity. The noise is in Stratonovich form (\(\circ\,dW\)), so the ordinary chain and product rules hold and the variational derivation goes through unchanged. Kelvin's theorem survives on loops carried by the stochastic flow, and all the Casimirs (for example helicity in 3D or enstrophy in 2D) are conserved. The energy, however, is not conserved in general.

Noise as a force: SFLT

In the paper we introduce a complementary construction, Stochastic Forcing by Lie Transport (SFLT). It starts from a stochastic version of the reduced Lagrange–d'Alembert–Pontryagin variational principle, in which noise enters as a prescribed stochastic force \(F_i\). This leads to the stochastically forced Lie–Poisson equation

$$ \mathrm{d}m + \mathrm{ad}^*_u m\,dt + \sum_i F_i \circ dW^i_t = 0, \qquad u = \frac{\delta h}{\delta m} . $$

The choice of \(F_i\) decides which conservation laws are kept. Choosing the force to be the Lie transport of prescribed one-form densities \(f_i\), that is \(F_i = \mathrm{ad}^*_u f_i\), gives an energy-preserving model:

$$ \mathrm{d}h = \Big\langle \mathrm{d}m, \frac{\delta h}{\delta m}\Big\rangle = \Big\langle -\mathrm{ad}^*_u m\,dt - \sum_i \mathrm{ad}^*_u f_i\circ dW^i_t,\; u\Big\rangle = 0, $$

because \(\mathrm{ad}_u u = -[u,u] = 0\). In vector notation, for incompressible flow the stochastic term is

$$ \sum_i \Big( \mathbf{u}\times \mathrm{curl}\,\mathbf{f}_i - \nabla(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{f}_i) \Big)\circ dW^i_t , $$

a stochastic vortex force of exactly the Craik–Leibovich type, plus a stochastic pressure gradient. Because the model comes from a variational principle, it still has a Kelvin–Noether circulation theorem. A different choice, \(F_i = \mathrm{ad}^*_{\delta C/\delta m} f_i\), preserves a chosen Casimir \(C\) (helicity, for instance) instead of the energy.

Waves and currents in the ocean mixed layer

Applying energy-preserving SFLT to the Euler–Boussinesq equations gives a stochastic extension of the Craik–Leibovich theory. Taking the first noise mode to be the steady Stokes drift, \(\mathbf{f}^0 = -\mathbf{u}^S(\mathbf{x})\) with \(dW^0_t = dt\), the equations become

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\mathbf{u} &= \mathbf{u}\times\mathrm{curl}\big(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{u}^S(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{x})\big)\,dt - \nabla\Big(p + \tfrac12|\mathbf{u}|^2 + \mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}^S\Big)dt \\ &\quad - gb\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}\,dt + \sum_{k>0}\Big(\mathbf{u}\times\mathrm{curl}\,\mathbf{f}^k - \nabla(\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{f}^k)\Big)\circ dW^k_t , \end{aligned} $$ $$ \partial_t D + \mathrm{div}(D\mathbf{u}) = 0 \ \text{ with } D = 1, \qquad \partial_t b + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla b = 0 , $$

where \(b\) is the buoyancy, \(p\) the pressure and \(2\boldsymbol{\Omega} = \mathrm{curl}\,\mathbf{R}\) the Coriolis parameter. The result has a clear physical reading: the Stokes drift itself becomes a semimartingale,

$$ \mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}^S = \mathbf{u}^S(\mathbf{x})\,dt + \sum_k \mathbf{f}^k(\mathbf{x})\circ dW^k_t , $$

so noise in the waves is passed on to the currents through a stochastic vortex force, while the total energy is unchanged. The spatial modes \(\mathbf{f}^k\) are to be calibrated from data, for example from high-resolution simulations or observations. When the noise terms are dropped, the deterministic CL equations are recovered.

In the full model, stochastic sources \(a^k\) can also be added to the buoyancy equation. They represent stochastic changes of material properties, such as the entrainment and detrainment of sediment, algae blooms, oil droplets or microplastics carried by Langmuir circulations. Circulation then obeys the stochastic Kelvin theorem

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{d}\oint_{c(u)}(\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{R})\cdot d\mathbf{x} &= \oint_{c(u)} gz\,\nabla b\cdot d\mathbf{x}\,dt \\ &\quad - \sum_k\oint_{c(u)}\Big((\mathrm{curl}\,\mathbf{f}^k)\times\mathbf{u} - gz\,\nabla a^k\Big)\cdot d\mathbf{x}\circ dW^k_t . \end{aligned} $$

The noise therefore creates circulation, locally and stochastically, which is how stochastic Langmuir circulations could arise. The potential vorticity \(q = (\boldsymbol{\omega} + 2\boldsymbol{\Omega})\cdot\nabla b\) obeys

$$ \mathrm{d}q + \mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla q\,dt = -\sum_k \mathrm{div}\,\mathbf{J}^k\circ dW^k_t , $$

a conservation law in flux form. Locally the stochastic "J-fluxes" redistribute potential vorticity, but with appropriate boundary conditions the total \(Q = \int_{\mathcal D} q\,dV\) is preserved.

Expectations and fluctuations

The paper also introduces an Eulerian-averaged version (EA SFLT), in which each solution is split into its expectation and a fluctuation. For quadratic Hamiltonians the expectation obeys a closed deterministic equation, and the energy of the expected flow decreases over time. That energy is transferred to the fluctuations, while the total energy stays exactly the same. This gives a conservative, geometric picture of how a mean flow loses energy to its variability, in the spirit of Lorenz's remark.

From models to computation

Stochastic models built this way inherit conservation laws and variational structure from their deterministic parents. Numerical methods should respect that structure too, otherwise the discretisation itself introduces spurious drift in energy, circulation or other invariants, which is indistinguishable from the physical noise we are trying to model. My later work develops structure-preserving discretisations for such models: stochastic multisymplectic integrators for stochastic PDEs, and symplectic methods for rough-path-driven adjoint systems used in sensitivity analysis and data assimilation.
